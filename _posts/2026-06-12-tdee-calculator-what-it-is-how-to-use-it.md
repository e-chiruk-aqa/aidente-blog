If you've searched for how many calories you should eat, you've probably seen the acronym TDEE. It stands for Total Daily Energy Expenditure, and it's one of the most useful numbers you can know when trying to lose weight, gain muscle, or simply stay where you are.

Here's what a TDEE calculator actually tells you — and how to turn that number into a calorie goal that works.

## What Is TDEE?

TDEE is the total number of calories your body burns in a single day — everything included. That means the energy your organs use just to keep you alive, the calories burned digesting food, and whatever you expend through movement, whether that's a structured workout or just walking to your car.

It's a single number that represents your full daily calorie burn. Eat exactly that amount and your weight stays stable. Eat less and you lose weight. Eat more and you gain.

The reason TDEE matters is that it's *your* number, not a generic guideline. "Eat 2,000 calories a day" means nothing in isolation. A sedentary 120-pound woman and an active 200-pound man have wildly different TDEEs, and the same calorie target will produce completely different outcomes for each of them.

## What Goes Into Your TDEE

A TDEE calculator combines two main components.

### BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate)

BMR is the number of calories your body burns at complete rest — just to keep your heart beating, lungs breathing, and temperature regulated. It accounts for roughly 60–75% of most people's total daily burn.

BMR is estimated from your height, weight, age, and biological sex. The most widely used formula is the Mifflin-St Jeor equation:

- **Men:** (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) + 5
- **Women:** (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) − 161

You don't need to do this by hand — any TDEE calculator handles it automatically.

### Activity Multiplier

BMR only accounts for rest. To arrive at TDEE, that number is multiplied by an activity factor that reflects how active you actually are day to day:

| Activity Level | Description | Multiplier |
|---|---|---|
| Sedentary | Desk job, little to no exercise | 1.2 |
| Lightly active | Light exercise 1–3 days/week | 1.375 |
| Moderately active | Moderate exercise 3–5 days/week | 1.55 |
| Very active | Hard exercise 6–7 days/week | 1.725 |
| Extremely active | Physical job plus daily intense training | 1.9 |

For example, a woman with a BMR of 1,400 calories who exercises three times a week would have a TDEE of roughly 1,400 × 1.55 = **2,170 calories per day**.

## How to Use Your TDEE to Set a Calorie Goal

Once you have your TDEE, setting a target is straightforward:

**To lose weight:** Eat below your TDEE. A deficit of 300–500 calories per day produces roughly 0.5–1 lb of loss per week, which research indicates is a sustainable pace that preserves muscle mass better than aggressive cuts.

**To maintain your weight:** Eat at your TDEE. This is your maintenance number — the level where your body is in energy balance.

**To gain muscle:** Eat slightly above your TDEE. A modest surplus of 200–300 calories is generally enough to support muscle building without adding significant fat alongside it.

The key word in all of this is "roughly." These numbers give you a calibrated starting point, not a guaranteed outcome.

## Why TDEE Calculators Aren't Perfect

No calculator can directly measure your metabolism — it estimates it based on population averages. Two people with identical stats can have TDEEs that differ by 10–15% due to genetics, hormonal differences, gut microbiome variation, and dozens of other factors that formulas can't capture.

The activity multiplier is also notoriously difficult to get right. Most people either underestimate how sedentary they are (picking "moderately active" when their primary exercise is occasional walking) or overestimate how much their workouts actually burn. Studies suggest that fitness trackers overestimate calorie burn by 15–40% on average — meaning eating back "earned" exercise calories can quietly erase a real deficit.

The solution is to treat TDEE as a starting estimate, then adjust based on what actually happens to your weight over two to three weeks of consistent tracking.

## Adjusting When the Numbers Don't Match Reality

**If you're eating at your calculated deficit but not losing weight:**
- Drop intake by 100–150 calories per day and reassess after two more weeks
- Audit your logging accuracy — underestimating portion sizes is the most common reason a calculated deficit doesn't show up on the scale

**If you're losing faster than expected and feeling drained:**
- Add 100–150 calories back, focusing on protein and complex carbohydrates
- Losing more than 1.5 lbs per week consistently is often a sign the deficit is larger than intended

**If progress stalled after months of steady loss:**
- Recalculate your TDEE from scratch. As your weight drops, your TDEE drops too. The number you calculated at 185 lbs won't be accurate when you reach 165 lbs.

Recalculating every 10–15 lbs of weight change is a good habit that keeps your targets accurate over time.

## The Most Practical Way to Work With TDEE

The most useful thing a TDEE calculator can do is give you a confident starting point rather than a magic answer. From there, consistent tracking is what turns the estimate into a number that actually fits your body — your real-world intake data is what closes the gap between population averages and your individual metabolism.
